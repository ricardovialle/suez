Calibration of null p-values

λ is the median null chi-square divided by its expected value: 1 when p-values are calibrated, above 1 when they are inflated (false positives), below 1 when deflated (lost power). Each dot is one dataset; the methods differ only in how they treat the two samples of each individual.

Share of genes with a planted effect found at gene level, after setting each method's threshold so its false-gene rate is 5% (the calibrated column). Datasets have 15 to 30 causal genes, so each value carries roughly ±10 to 15 percentage points of noise: read the pattern, not single cells.

Modelling

Smooth conditions

For effects that change with age or dose, a spline condition finds onsets and mid-life peaks that a linear term misses, at no cost in power for linear effects and with better-calibrated null p-values. Details in the age-varying eQTL report.

When suez is the better tool

  • Repeated samples of the same individuals, two or more conditions, or unbalanced designs.
  • Response eQTLs as the question, including continuous and smooth conditions.
  • Related individuals or strong hidden structure, modelled in the same covariance.
  • Admixed cohorts with ancestry-specific effects.

When tensorQTL is the better tool

  • One sample per unrelated individual: a linear regression is calibrated and very fast.
  • Dense GRM kernels at 1,000+ samples, where suez was 2 to 4 times slower.
  • trans scans and many permutations: suez maps cis only.
  • Mature downstream tooling: gene-level permutation p-values, conditional signals, fine-mapping inputs.
